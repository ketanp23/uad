WP 01
Departure · Sensing physics

Aerial Remote Sensing Data

Remote sensing is the art of learning about a surface without touching it — by measuring the electromagnetic radiation it reflects or emits. A UAV is simply a very low, very flexible remote-sensing platform: it flies under the clouds, on demand, and resolves centimetres where a satellite resolves metres.

Remote sensing = acquiring information about an object or area by measuring reflected or emitted electromagnetic (EM) energy from a distance, then processing and interpreting that measurement.
Passive sensing
  • Energy source = the Sun (reflected light) or the object's own heat (emitted thermal radiation).
  • Sensors = RGB cameras, multispectral cameras, hyperspectral imagers, thermal (TIR) cameras.
  • Limits = needs illumination (except thermal), affected by shadows, haze and time of day.
Active sensing
  • Energy source = the sensor itself fires energy and measures the echo.
  • Sensors = LiDAR (laser pulses), radar / SAR (microwaves).
  • Strengths = works day or night, measures range directly, LiDAR sees through gaps in vegetation.
Band explorer Click a spectral band
The four resolutions
  • Spatial = size of one pixel on the ground (GSD). UAV: 0.5–5 cm. Satellite: 30 cm – 30 m.
  • Spectral = how many bands, and how narrow. RGB = 3 wide bands; multispectral ≈ 4–10; hyperspectral = 100+ narrow contiguous bands.
  • Radiometric = bit depth — how many brightness levels per band (8-bit = 256, 12-bit = 4096).
  • Temporal = revisit time. Satellites: days. UAVs: whenever you choose to fly — the decisive advantage for monitoring.
Exam trap. "High resolution" alone is meaningless — always state which of the four resolutions you mean.

Healthy leaves absorb red (chlorophyll) and strongly reflect NIR (cell structure). NDVI exploits the gap:

WP 02
Active ranging

LiDAR Data

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) fires hundreds of thousands of laser pulses per second and times each echo. Because every point's position comes from the sensor's own measurement — not from matching photographs — LiDAR delivers 3D structure even over textureless surfaces and through gaps in vegetation down to the true ground.

Range from time of flight: R = c · Δt / 2 — half, because the pulse travels out and back at the speed of light c.
One pulse, many returns
  • Discrete-return = the scanner records 2–5 echo peaks per pulse. Standard on UAV LiDAR.
  • Full-waveform = the entire returning energy curve is digitised — richer vegetation structure, heavier data.
  • Direct georeferencing = each point = scanner range + angle, combined with GNSS position and IMU attitude, all time-synchronised. No photo matching needed.
  • Intensity = strength of each echo — a free "grayscale image" that helps classify asphalt vs grass vs paint.
Remember. LiDAR accuracy depends on the GNSS/IMU trajectory quality; photogrammetry accuracy depends on image texture, overlap and GCPs.

Data Processing Approaches

Raw UAV data is just gigabytes of photographs or laser echoes. Processing is the pipeline that turns them into measurable geospatial products: point clouds, surface models, orthomosaics and maps that survive in a GIS.

Photogrammetry pipeline · SfM–MVS Step through the stages
LiDAR processing chain
  • 1 · Trajectory = post-process the GNSS + IMU log (PPK) into a precise flight path.
  • 2 · Point generation = combine trajectory + scanner angles/ranges → raw georeferenced cloud.
  • 3 · Strip adjustment = align overlapping flight lines; calibrate boresight angles.
  • 4 · Filtering = remove noise points (birds, multipath, air returns).
  • 5 · Classification = separate ground from vegetation and buildings (e.g. cloth-simulation filter, progressive TIN).
  • 6 · Products = DTM from ground class, DSM from first returns, contours, sections, CHM.

WP 04
Position truth

Georeferencing Approach

A beautiful orthomosaic sitting 3 m away from where the world actually is, is a picture — not a map. Georeferencing ties every pixel and point to a real coordinate reference system (CRS), and the method you choose sets the accuracy budget of the whole project.

Georeferencing = assigning real-world coordinates (e.g. WGS-84 / UTM zone 44N for Nagpur) to image pixels or cloud points. Indirect = tie to measured ground points. Direct = trust a precisely corrected on-board position.
Method selector Click to compare

BAR = TYPICAL ABSOLUTE ACCURACY (LOG SCALE) · SHORTER IS BETTER

GCP field craft
  • What = ground control points: targets visible from the air, surveyed with RTK GNSS or a total station to cm accuracy.
  • How many = 5–10 for a typical site; more for long corridors or large height variation.
  • Where = spread to the edges and centre, at different elevations — never clustered in one corner.
  • Checkpoints = extra surveyed points not used in processing — held back to independently report accuracy (RMSE).
RTK vs PPK, in one breath
  • RTK = Real-Time Kinematic — correction stream (base station / NTRIP) applied in flight; needs a live radio/data link.
  • PPK = Post-Processed Kinematic — raw GNSS logged on the drone and base, corrected after landing; immune to link dropouts, usually the more robust survey choice.
  • Both = bring camera positions to 2–5 cm, cutting GCP needs to a few check points.
  • Detail = the antenna–camera lever arm and precise exposure timestamps must be known, or the centimetres evaporate.

Cloud Computing Approaches

A single survey can produce 20–100 GB of images and clouds, and dense matching is brutally parallel — perfect GPU work. The question is where that compute lives: on the drone, on your desk, or in a data centre.

Compute location trade-off Click an approach
Why teams choose cloud (SaaS)
  • Elastic scale = a 10,000-image job rents 50 GPUs for an hour instead of a week on one workstation.
  • No capex = subscription replaces a ₹3–5 lakh GPU rig that ages in three years.
  • Collaboration = results live at a URL: annotate, measure, share with the client the same day.
  • Pipelines = automatic processing on upload, APIs, versioned projects.
…and why they hesitate
  • Bandwidth = uploading 60 GB over a site 4G link can take longer than the processing.
  • Data governance = survey data of critical infrastructure may not be allowed off-premises or out of country.
  • Recurring cost = subscriptions outgrow a one-time licence for heavy continuous users.
  • Control = fewer knobs than desktop software when a dataset misbehaves.

Image Processing Approaches

From here to WP 15 we go inside a single image. A digital image is nothing mystical: a matrix of numbers. Every operation you will meet — filtering, edges, histograms, segmentation — is arithmetic on that matrix.

A grayscale image is a function f(x, y) sampled on a grid: rows × cols pixels, each an intensity (0 = black … 255 = white for 8-bit). A colour image stacks three such matrices (channels).
Three levels of processing
  • Low level = image → image. Denoising, contrast, sharpening (WP 11, 13).
  • Mid level = image → features. Edges, contours, segments, keypoints (WP 12, 14).
  • High level = features → meaning. "That blob is a vehicle" (WP 15, and deep learning beyond this unit).
Operation families
  • Point = each output pixel depends only on the same input pixel (brightness, threshold).
  • Local = depends on a neighbourhood (convolution filters, median).
  • Global = depends on the whole image (histogram equalisation, Fourier).
  • Geometric = moves pixels (resize, rotate, warp, orthorectification itself!).

Exploring Color Space

A colour space is a coordinate system for colour. RGB is how sensors capture and screens emit; HSV separates what colour from how bright — which is why segmentation and tracking live there.

Channel viewer Rendered live from the scene
Input · BGR scene

The spaces that matter
  • BGR / RGB = additive device space. Intuitive, but brightness and colour are tangled across all three channels.
  • Grayscale = one channel: 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B (green dominates because eyes do).
  • HSV = Hue (colour angle), Saturation (purity), Value (brightness). In OpenCV: H ∈ 0–179 (degrees ÷ 2 to fit uint8!), S,V ∈ 0–255.
  • L*a*b* = L = lightness, a/b = colour opponents. Perceptually uniform — CLAHE (WP 13) is applied to L so colours don't shift.
hsv  = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)
gray = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
lab  = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2LAB)

Image Filters

A filter slides a small matrix — a kernel — across the image; each output pixel is the weighted sum of its neighbourhood. This one idea (convolution) gives you blurring, sharpening, and (next waypoint) edge detection.

Do the noise test. Turn the noise on, then compare Gaussian vs median: Gaussian smears every speck into a grey smudge, the median simply votes them away. Nonlinear beats linear on impulse noise — a guaranteed exam point.

Edge Detection

An edge is a fast change in intensity — a large gradient. Derivative kernels estimate that gradient; Canny then cleans it into thin, connected, trustworthy lines. Watch the road and roof outlines emerge from the field below.

Canny in five steps
  • 1 · Smooth = Gaussian blur — derivatives amplify noise, so remove it first.
  • 2 · Gradient = Sobel Gx, Gy → magnitude and direction per pixel.
  • 3 · Non-maximum suppression = keep only pixels that are the local peak across the gradient direction → 1-pixel-thin edges.
  • 4 · Double threshold = ≥ high → strong edge; between low and high → weak candidate; below low → discarded.
  • 5 · Hysteresis = keep weak pixels only if they connect to a strong one — broken edges heal, isolated noise dies.
Calls & rules of thumb
gx = cv2.Sobel(gray, cv2.CV_64F, 1, 0, ksize=3)
gy = cv2.Sobel(gray, cv2.CV_64F, 0, 1, ksize=3)
lap = cv2.Laplacian(gray, cv2.CV_64F)      # 2nd derivative

edges = cv2.Canny(gray, 40, 100)           # low, high
  • Threshold ratio = keep high : low around 2:1 to 3:1 (Canny's own advice).
  • Laplacian = one kernel, finds edges of any orientation, but noise-hungry — pair with Gaussian ("LoG").
  • Why CV_64F = gradients go negative; uint8 would silently clip them.

Histogram, Equalisation & CLAHE

A histogram is the census of an image: how many pixels sit at each brightness. Hazy aerial imagery piles its pixels into a narrow band — equalisation spreads them out, and CLAHE does it locally, with a clip so noise isn't amplified.

Contrast bench The scene, deliberately hazed — fix it

4 × 4
Input · low-contrast (hazy)
—
Histogram of output · magenta = counts · blue line = cumulative (CDF)
Global equalisation vs CLAHE
  • equalizeHist = one mapping for the whole image, built from the global CDF. Great when the whole frame is uniformly dull; can blow out skies and amplify flat-area noise.
  • CLAHE = Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation. The image is split into tiles (default 8×8); each tile gets its own mapping; the clip limit caps any histogram bin before the CDF is built, and clipped counts are redistributed — limiting noise gain. Tile mappings are blended by bilinear interpolation, so no blocky seams.
  • Colour rule = never equalise B, G, R separately (colours shift). Convert to LAB, enhance L only, convert back.
Calls
hist = cv2.calcHist([gray], [0], None, [256], [0, 256])

eq = cv2.equalizeHist(gray)               # global

clahe = cv2.createCLAHE(clipLimit=2.5,
                        tileGridSize=(8, 8))
better = clahe.apply(gray)                # local, clipped

# colour-safe version:
lab = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2LAB)
l, a, b = cv2.split(lab)
lab = cv2.merge((clahe.apply(l), a, b))
out = cv2.cvtColor(lab, cv2.COLOR_LAB2BGR)
In the lab above, raise the clip limit and watch texture (and noise) intensify; raise the tile count and watch enhancement become more local. That is the entire CLAHE trade-off.

Segmentation approaches
  • Global threshold = cv2.threshold(gray, T, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY); add + cv2.THRESH_OTSU to pick T automatically from a bimodal histogram.
  • Adaptive threshold = a different T per neighbourhood — for uneven illumination across a frame.
  • Colour segmentation = cv2.inRange(hsv, lo, hi) — the vegetation mask from WP 10.
  • Watershed = treats gradient as terrain and "floods" from markers — separates touching objects.
  • GrabCut = iterative graph-cut foreground extraction from a rough rectangle.
  • K-means = cluster pixels by colour into k segments.
Contour toolkit
_, binary = cv2.threshold(gray, 0, 255,
        cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)

cnts, hier = cv2.findContours(binary,
        cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL,          # outermost only (RETR_TREE = nested)
        cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)    # compress straight runs

for c in cnts:
    A = cv2.contourArea(c)
    P = cv2.arcLength(c, True)
    x, y, w, h = cv2.boundingRect(c)
    M = cv2.moments(c)              # centroid = (M10/M00, M01/M00)

  • UAV uses = counting trees/panels, measuring stockpile footprints, locating landing pads by shape.

Haar Cascade

The Viola–Jones Haar cascade (2001) made real-time object detection possible on plain CPUs — which is why it still matters on compute-starved UAV companion boards. Its three tricks: rectangle features, the integral image, and a cascade that rejects boring windows early.

Trick 1 & 2 · Features + integral image
  • Integral image = each cell stores the sum of everything above-left. Any rectangle's sum then costs exactly 4 lookups — so a 24×24 window can evaluate 160,000+ candidate features cheaply.
  • AdaBoost = picks the few thousand most discriminative features and weights them into strong stage classifiers.
Trick 3 · The cascade Interactive funnel

A sliding window scans every position and scale. Almost all windows are sky and grass — early cheap stages throw them out; only survivors face the expensive later stages.

Using a pretrained cascade
face = cv2.CascadeClassifier(
    cv2.data.haarcascades + "haarcascade_frontalface_default.xml")

gray = cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
hits = face.detectMultiScale(gray,
        scaleFactor=1.1,     # image shrink step between scales
        minNeighbors=5,      # overlapping hits needed → fewer false +
        minSize=(30, 30))

for (x, y, w, h) in hits:
    cv2.rectangle(frame, (x, y), (x+w, y+h), (255, 0, 0), 2)
  • scaleFactor = smaller (1.05) = finds more sizes, slower; larger (1.3) = faster, may skip.
  • minNeighbors = raise to suppress false positives, lower to catch difficult targets.
  • Shipped models = frontal face, eyes, full body, smile — and you can train custom cascades (e.g. cars) from positive/negative samples.
Verdict for UAV work
  • Pros = real-time on CPU, tiny model files (XML), no GPU — ideal for onboard person/vehicle spotting in search-and-rescue video.
  • Cons = mostly frontal/upright targets, sensitive to rotation and lighting, more false positives than modern detectors.
  • Context = superseded in accuracy by HOG+SVM and deep networks (YOLO, SSD) — but it remains the canonical example of classical, explainable detection, and the exam's favourite.

Rapid Revision & Self-Check

One-line recall
  • Remote sensing = measuring reflected/emitted EM energy from a distance; passive (sun/heat) vs active (LiDAR, radar).
  • Four resolutions = spatial (GSD), spectral (bands), radiometric (bit depth), temporal (revisit).
  • NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red); dense healthy vegetation ≳ 0.6.
  • LiDAR range = R = c·Δt/2; multiple returns per pulse; last return ≈ ground → DTM under canopy.
  • Point density = PRR / (speed × swath), swath = 2·h·tan(FOV/2); stored as LAS/LAZ with class codes (2 = ground, 6 = building).
  • SfM–MVS = features → matching → bundle adjustment (sparse + poses) → dense cloud → mesh/DSM → orthomosaic.
  • CHM = DSM − DTM.
  • Georeferencing = indirect (GCPs) vs direct (RTK live / PPK after landing); checkpoints report RMSE honestly.
  • Cloud choice = edge (real-time, tiny), workstation (control, capex), cloud SaaS (elastic, upload-bound), hybrid.
  • OpenCV I/O = imread returns a BGR NumPy array (None on failure); video = VideoCapture read-loop + VideoWriter(fourcc).
  • HSV = hue 0–179 in OpenCV; segment colour with inRange; CLAHE goes on L of LAB.
  • Filters = Gaussian for general smoothing; median for salt & pepper; filter2D for custom kernels like sharpen.
  • Canny = smooth → gradient → non-max suppression → double threshold (≈1:2–1:3) → hysteresis.
  • CLAHE = tiled equalisation + clip limit + bilinear blending — local contrast without runaway noise.
  • Contours = findContours on a binary image; area/perimeter/boundingRect/moments per contour.
  • Haar = rectangle features + integral image (4 lookups) + AdaBoost + attentional cascade; detectMultiScale(scaleFactor, minNeighbors).

Key Takeaways

If you remember only one thing

Data collection and processing are one continuous chain of custody. The sensor decides what physics you record, georeferencing decides whether it is a map or merely a picture, the pipeline decides what products exist — and every OpenCV operation afterwards is just disciplined arithmetic on a matrix whose numbers you now know how to trust.

01

Sensors are physics choices

RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral and thermal read reflected or emitted energy (passive); LiDAR and radar bring their own (active). Choose the band that carries your signal — NIR for vegetation vigour, TIR for heat, laser for structure.

02

Say which resolution you mean

Spatial, spectral, radiometric, temporal. A UAV wins on spatial (cm GSD) and temporal (fly on demand); satellites win on coverage and spectral breadth.

03

LiDAR measures, photogrammetry infers

Each LiDAR point is a direct range measurement, georeferenced by GNSS+IMU — texture and lighting are irrelevant, and last returns find ground under canopy. Photogrammetry reconstructs from image matching and lives or dies on texture and overlap.

04

Density is a budget, not a wish

Points per square metre = pulse rate divided by (speed × swath). Fly lower and slower for powerlines; higher and faster for districts. You cannot have density and coverage from the same battery.

05

Know the SfM stages in order

Features → matches → bundle adjustment (poses + sparse cloud) → dense MVS → mesh/DSM → orthomosaic. Georeferencing enters via GCP marks or RTK/PPK camera positions before densification.

06

CHM = DSM − DTM

Surface minus terrain equals what grows or stands on the terrain. Three products, one subtraction, endless exam questions.

07

Accuracy has to be proven

GCPs constrain the solution; independent checkpoints report RMSE. RTK corrects in flight, PPK after landing — PPK forgives link dropouts, which is why surveyors love it.

08

Compute placement is a trade

Edge gives real-time reaction with tiny power; workstations give control; cloud gives elastic GPUs and collaboration but demands bandwidth and trust. Hybrid triages on board and crunches in the data centre.

09

Software names win easy marks

Photogrammetry: Metashape, Pix4D, DroneDeploy, OpenDroneMap, RealityCapture. LiDAR: DJI Terra, TerraSolid, LiDAR360, LAStools, CloudCompare, PDAL. Know one open-source option in each list.

10

An image is a matrix — and it's BGR

imread returns a uint8 NumPy array, channels ordered blue-green-red, shape (rows, cols, 3), None on failure. Video is a loop of such matrices at some fps.

11

Pick the space for the job

HSV separates colour from brightness — masks survive lighting changes. LAB separates lightness — CLAHE goes on L. Gray is enough for edges, contours and Haar.

12

Match the filter to the noise

Gaussian for general smoothing before derivatives; median for salt & pepper; bilateral when edges must survive. Sharpening is just a kernel with a positive centre and negative ring.

13

Canny is a pipeline, not a kernel

Smooth, differentiate, thin (non-max suppression), double-threshold, connect (hysteresis). The 2–3× threshold ratio and the hysteresis step are what make its edges clean.

14

Binary first, then contours

Threshold (Otsu for bimodal, adaptive for uneven light, inRange for colour) turns the image binary; findContours then hands you countable, measurable objects — area, perimeter, centroid, bounding box.

15

The cascade spends effort where it matters

Haar features cost four integral-image lookups; AdaBoost selects them; the cascade rejects empty windows in the first cheap stages. That asymmetry — most of the frame is nothing — is why it runs in real time on a drone's CPU.

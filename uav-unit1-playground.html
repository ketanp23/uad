Departure

Introduction to UAVs & Drones

An Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) is a powered aircraft that carries no human pilot on board, flies under remote control or by its own onboard autonomy, can be expendable or recoverable, and can carry a payload. The pilot is not removed from the loop — only from the airframe.

UAV vs UAS vs RPAS vs drone. The vehicle is only one component. The full system — vehicle, ground control station, data links, payload, launch/recovery equipment and human operators — is the UAS (Unmanned Aircraft System). ICAO prefers RPAS (Remotely Piloted Aircraft System) for aircraft always under a licensed remote pilot. "Drone" is the popular, non-technical umbrella term.

The five subsystems of a UAS click a block
Why unmanned
  • Dull — long endurance surveys, pipeline and border patrol where a human pilot fatigues.
  • Dirty — radiation, chemical plumes, volcanic ash, post-disaster smoke.
  • Dangerous — combat, wildfire, structural inspection at height, confined spaces.
  • Cheap & small — no cockpit, life support, or crew safety margin, so the airframe shrinks and cost drops.
  • Precise — repeatable, GPS-locked flight lines that a human cannot fly by hand.
Brief timeline
  • 1849 Austrian balloon bombs over Venice — the first unmanned aerial attack.
  • 1917–18 Kettering Bug and the Ruston Proctor Aerial Target: first powered UAVs.
  • 1930s–60s Radio-controlled target drones (DH.82 Queen Bee — origin of "drone"); Ryan Firebee reconnaissance.
  • 1980s–90s IAI Scout / Pioneer prove tactical ISR; Predator enters service 1995.
  • 2010s Cheap MEMS IMUs, brushless motors, LiPo cells and open-source autopilots create the civil multirotor market.
  • 2020s BVLOS corridors, delivery trials, drone swarms, Remote ID, UTM.
Exam line — Remember the ordering: UAV ⊂ UAS. A question that says "the UAV consists of ground control station and data link" is wrong; that is the UAS.

Multirotor UAV Systems

A multirotor has no swashplate and no control surfaces. Every axis is flown by differential thrust alone — the flight controller changes individual motor speeds hundreds of times per second. Build a frame below and command it.

How each axis is produced
  • Throttle All motors up equally → climb. Total thrust vs weight.
  • Roll One lateral side speeds up, the other slows → bank, then translate sideways.
  • Pitch Front pair vs rear pair differential → nose down, aircraft accelerates forward.
  • Yaw Speed up all CCW props and slow all CW props (or vice-versa). Net aerodynamic torque imbalance turns the airframe — thrust stays constant, so altitude holds.

Mixer form: Mᵢ = T − roll·xᵢ − pitch·yᵢ + yaw·σᵢ, where (xᵢ,yᵢ) is the motor position and σᵢ = +1 for CCW, −1 for CW.

Why props alternate direction

Each spinning propeller drags air and reacts against the airframe with an equal and opposite torque. If all four props spun the same way, the body would spin continuously the other way. Half CW and half CCW cancels net torque in hover; deliberately unbalancing that cancellation is exactly how yaw is commanded.

Also note: a tricopter cannot cancel torque with 3 motors, so it adds a servo that tilts the tail motor to vector thrust for yaw.

Frame class = motor-to-motor diagonal, mm 5-inch = 210–250 mm Prop = diameter × pitch
Multirotor strengths & limits
  • + VTOL no runway, launches from a footpath.
  • + Hover station-keeping over a single asset for inspection.
  • + Mechanically simple no gearbox, swashplate or linkages; motors are the only moving parts.
  • + Agile high thrust-to-weight, instant attitude response.
  • − Endurance all lift is bought with power; 15–40 min typical.
  • − Efficiency no wing means no free lift in cruise.
  • − Payload fraction usually 10–30 % of MTOW.
  • − Wind & noise small props are loud and gust-sensitive.

Payload selection rules
  • Payload fraction 10–30 % of MTOW for multirotors; every extra 100 g of sensor costs both endurance and control margin.
  • Centre of gravity must stay on the thrust axis — an off-CG payload makes the FC fight a permanent trim offset and burns current.
  • Vibration isolation rolling-shutter cameras and IMU-coupled sensors need damped mounts, or you get jello and blurred imagery.
  • Gimbal 2-axis (pitch/roll) for mapping, 3-axis (adds yaw/pan) for cinematography and tracking.
  • Power & data budget LiDAR and hyperspectral can draw 20–60 W and produce data faster than any radio link can downstream — so they record on board.
  • Legal payload determines whether the flight is "commercial", and cameras trigger privacy law.
Consumable vs non-consumable

Non-consumable (dispensable) payloads return with the aircraft — cameras, LiDAR, radios, radar. Mass is constant, so the trim is constant.

Consumable payloads are released in flight — agricultural spray, seeds, delivery parcels, medical supplies, marker dye. Mass falls during the mission, which improves endurance but shifts the CG and changes handling, so spray drones use flow-rate compensation and tank baffles.

Links, protocols & failsafes
  • C2 link Command & control uplink: 2.4 GHz RC, or LTE/5G for BVLOS.
  • Telemetry MAVLink over 433/868/915 MHz radios, or over IP; carries heartbeat, attitude, GPS, battery at 1–10 Hz.
  • Video analogue 5.8 GHz FPV (lowest latency) or digital HD / RTMP over cellular (higher latency, better quality).
  • GCS software Mission Planner, QGroundControl — map, HUD, parameter editor, log download.
  • Failsafes RC loss → hold/RTL; GCS loss → continue mission or RTL; low battery → land; geofence breach → stop at the boundary; GPS loss → switch to altitude-hold on baro + optical flow.
  • Regulatory monitoring Remote ID broadcast, and in India the Digital Sky / NPNT ("No Permission, No Take-off") workflow with logged flight authorisation.

The data pipeline click a stage
Flight patterns
  • Lawnmower / grid parallel lines over an area — the default for orthomosaics.
  • Double grid a second grid at 90°, plus camera tilt — needed for 3D building models.
  • Corridor a narrow strip following roads, rivers, pipelines, power lines.
  • Orbit / point of interest circles a target at fixed radius and camera angle — towers, stacks, monuments.
  • Facade scan vertical raster along a wall for structural inspection.
  • Terrain-following AGL held constant over a DEM so GSD stays uniform on slopes.
Getting position right
  • Geotagging each image is stamped with GNSS position + IMU attitude at shutter time.
  • GCPs Ground Control Points — surveyed targets on the ground; the gold standard for absolute accuracy.
  • RTK real-time kinematic correction from a base station → cm-level positions in flight.
  • PPK post-processed kinematic — same accuracy, corrections applied after landing, no live radio link needed.
  • Checkpoints extra surveyed points held back from processing to measure the achieved accuracy.
Trigger modes — Time-lapse (simple, overlap varies with speed) vs distance-triggered (constant overlap, preferred) vs waypoint-triggered (exact repeatability for change detection).

Challenges of UAV Networks

Put several UAVs on one network and you get a FANET — a Flying Ad-hoc Network. It inherits every problem of a mobile ad-hoc network and adds three-dimensional movement at 30–100 km/h, a severe energy budget, and nodes that go out of range constantly.

Rapid Revision & Self-Check

One-line recall
  • UAS = UAV (the aircraft) + GCS (ground control station) + data link + payload + launch/recovery gear + crew.
  • India weight bands = 250 g / 2 kg / 25 kg / 150 kg boundaries → nano, micro, small, medium, large.
  • NATO = Class I (<150 kg: micro/mini/small), Class II (150–600 kg), Class III (>600 kg).
  • Altitude ladder = LASE (low altitude, short endurance) → LALE (low altitude, long endurance) → MALE (medium altitude, long endurance) → HALE (high altitude, long endurance) → HAPS (high altitude pseudo-satellite).
  • Airframes = fixed-wing, single-rotor, multirotor, hybrid VTOL (vertical take-off and landing), flapping-wing, lighter-than-air.
  • Multirotor control = differential thrust only; yaw comes from the torque imbalance between the CW (clockwise) and CCW (counter-clockwise) propellers.
  • Power chain = LiPo (lithium-polymer) battery → PDB (power distribution board) → ESC (electronic speed controller) → BLDC (brushless DC) motor → propeller; the BEC (battery eliminator circuit) taps 5 V for the FC (flight controller).
  • Core sensors = gyroscope + accelerometer together forming the IMU (inertial measurement unit), magnetometer (compass), barometer (pressure altitude), GNSS (satellite positioning), optionally lidar or optical flow.
  • Payload trade = mass vs power vs data rate vs stabilisation.
  • Monitoring = MAVLink telemetry + video downlink into a GCS, with layered failsafes such as RTL (return to launch).
  • GSD (ground sample distance) = (sensor width × altitude × 100) / (focal length × image width) cm per pixel.
  • FANET (flying ad-hoc network) pain = high 3D mobility, sparse nodes, short-lived links, tight energy, weak security.

Key Takeaways

If you remember only one thing

A drone is not a flying camera — it is a system in which every choice is a trade against a fixed energy budget. Weight decides the law, lift method decides endurance, differential thrust decides control, the payload decides the mission, and the radio link decides how much of any of it you can actually see and command from the ground.

01

The vehicle is one part of the system

UAV (unmanned aerial vehicle) means the aircraft alone. UAS (unmanned aircraft system) adds the ground control station, the data links, the payload, the launch and recovery method, and the trained crew. Exam answers that confuse the two lose marks immediately.

02

Mass is the legal axis because energy is

Impact energy rises with mass, so regulators split drones at 250 g, 2 kg, 25 kg and 150 kg — nano, micro, small, medium, large under India's Drone Rules 2021. The military NATO scale (Class I / II / III) is the same idea drawn at different boundaries.

03

Classification depends on the question

The same aircraft can be classed by weight, range, altitude, endurance, wing type, autonomy or application. Read which axis is being asked about before answering — LASE to HAPS is an altitude ladder, not a weight one.

04

Lift method sets everything downstream

Fixed-wing buys endurance and area coverage but cannot hover. Multirotors buy hover and precision but burn their battery just to stay airborne. Hybrid VTOL buys both and pays in mass, cost and a risky transition moment.

05

Multirotors fly on differential thrust alone

No swashplate, no control surfaces. Roll and pitch come from speeding one side up and the other down; yaw comes from unbalancing the reaction torque between CW (clockwise) and CCW (counter-clockwise) propellers, which is exactly why they alternate direction.

06

Motor count buys redundancy, not endurance

A quadcopter that loses one motor cannot hold heading and comes down. A hexacopter survives one failure and an octocopter two — which is why expensive payloads and flights near people use six or eight motors, despite the extra weight and current draw.

07

Follow the energy through the power chain

LiPo battery → power module → PDB (power distribution board) → ESC (electronic speed controller) → BLDC (brushless DC) motor → propeller. The ESC's whole job is turning DC into the three-phase AC waveform a brushless motor needs.

08

The control lane is a loop, not a chain

IMU (inertial measurement unit), magnetometer, barometer and GNSS feed the FC (flight controller). Its EKF (extended Kalman filter) fuses them into one estimate of where the aircraft is and how it is oriented, the control law issues motor commands, and the aircraft's response returns through the same sensors.

09

Payload is a four-way trade

Mass, power, data rate and stabilisation. Every extra 100 g costs endurance and control margin; sensors like LiDAR and hyperspectral generate data faster than any radio can send it, so they record on board and are processed after landing.

10

Thrust-to-weight below 2 : 1 is not flyable work

Hovering consumes the thrust equal to the weight; everything above that is your margin for climbing, correcting and fighting gusts. Two-to-one is the practical floor for steady camera work, three or four to one for agile or windy flying.

11

Monitoring is two live streams plus failsafes

Telemetry down (MAVLink over a 433/915 MHz radio or cellular) and command up (C2, the command and control link), displayed in a GCS. Layered failsafes — RTL (return to launch), land, geofence stop — decide what happens when either stream breaks.

12

Survey planning runs backwards from accuracy

Required accuracy fixes GSD (ground sample distance), GSD fixes altitude, altitude and overlap fix the image count, and image count fixes the battery plan. Doubling altitude doubles GSD but roughly quarters the number of photographs.

13

Position accuracy comes from corrections, not the drone

A bare GNSS fix is metres out. GCPs (ground control points), RTK (real-time kinematic) corrections in flight, or PPK (post-processed kinematic) corrections after landing are what turn an image set into a survey.

14

FANETs break conventional networking

Nodes move fast in three dimensions, sit far apart, hold links for seconds, and spend the same battery on flying and transmitting. Routing must be position-aware and energy-aware; ordinary MANET protocols simply cannot keep up.

15

Security and airspace are the real blockers

Civil GNSS is unauthenticated and many links are unencrypted, so jamming, spoofing and hijack are live threats. Combined with detect-and-avoid limits and shifting national rules, this — not battery chemistry — is what currently gates routine BVLOS (beyond visual line of sight) operation.
